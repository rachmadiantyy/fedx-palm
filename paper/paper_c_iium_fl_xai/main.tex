% --- ABSTRACT (English) — REQUIRED
\noindent\textbf{ABSTRACT:} Automated oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) ripeness
detection promises consistency over subjective manual grading, but centralizing
plantation imagery conflicts with the confidentiality of operational data, and
deep models offer no formal privacy by default. This paper presents FedX-Palm,
a framework that integrates a YOLOv11n--GroupNorm detector with horizontal
\emph{federated learning} (FL), per-sample \emph{differential privacy} (DP),
and Grad-CAM++ explainability for six-class FFB ripeness detection, and
empirically maps where the combination is practical and where it is not.
Experiments use a leakage-free split keyed by bunch identity (9,094 training,
769 validation, 951 test images from 89 bunches) with Non-IID Dirichlet client
partitions. Without privacy noise, the federated model attains mAP@0.5 = 0.738,
only 0.049 below the centralized reference (0.787) -- so architectural FL
offers near-centralized accuracy. We then characterize the privacy--utility
trade-off over a $5 \times 5$ grid of clients $K \in \{2,4,8,12,16\}$ and noise
multipliers $\sigma \in \{0.5,1,1.5,2,3\}$, with per-client $\varepsilon$
derived from a privacy-loss-distribution (PRV) accountant at $\delta =
10^{-5}$. Under full-model DP-SGD the best configuration reaches mAP@0.5 =
0.190 ($\varepsilon = 8.36$); degradation is gradual rather than a cliff,
larger $K$ consistently worsens utility (a per-sample-DP penalty opposite to
client-level DP), and freezing the backbone (partial DP) does not help in this
new-domain setting. Three operating regimes emerge -- no-formal-privacy,
weak-DP, and strict-DP -- with the federated no-DP model the only deployable
point at present. Grad-CAM++ faithfulness on that operating model gives global
Focus Retention Rate (FRR) = 0.281 versus 0.182 centralized, so federation
does not degrade explanation quality. The deployable checkpoint is packaged
into a Docker image and demonstrated as a CPU-only inference service on a
Virtual Private Server, returning bounding boxes, Grad-CAM++ heatmaps, and
per-detection summaries at roughly two seconds per image. The results
delineate where federated FFB detection is practical today and where formal DP
remains a research target rather than a deployable option.

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
The oil palm industry is a cornerstone of Indonesian and Malaysian
agribusiness, with the two countries together accounting for over 85\% of the
world's palm oil supply~\cite{usda2024}. At the operational level, the single
most influential decision in determining oil yield and quality is the timing of
fresh fruit bunch (FFB) harvest. Bunches harvested too early contain
insufficient mesocarp oil, whereas bunches harvested too late accumulate Free
Fatty Acid (FFA), degrading Crude Palm Oil (CPO) quality. Industry practice
distinguishes six commercially relevant ripeness classes -- \emph{Unripe},
\emph{Underripe}, \emph{Ripe}, \emph{Overripe}, \emph{Empty Bunch}, and
\emph{Abnormal} -- and traditional assessment by harvest foremen, based on bunch
colour and loose fruitlet count, is subjective with reported misclassification
rates of 15--25\% under suboptimal lighting~\cite{sabri2017}.

Modern object detectors of the YOLO family~\cite{redmon2016}, of which
YOLOv11~\cite{yolov11} is the latest iteration, can localize and classify
multiple bunches in a single frame in real time. Several studies have applied
them to palm imagery~\cite{junos2022,gunawan2023,elwirehardja2021}, yet two
practical barriers remain. First, training accurate detectors requires diverse
imagery spanning seasons, lighting, and sites; centralizing such imagery clashes
with a reality that plantation images are commercially sensitive -- revealing
block locations, production volumes, and operational practices -- so estates are
reluctant to ship raw data to a shared server. Second, deep models leak
information about their training data through their parameters, so collaborative
training without a formal privacy mechanism is not, by itself, privacy
preserving.

\emph{Federated learning} (FL)~\cite{fedavg} answers the first barrier
architecturally: multiple estates collaboratively train a shared model while raw
imagery remains local; only model parameters traverse the network. In the
taxonomy of Kairouz \emph{et al.}~\cite{kairouz2021}, the plantation-consortium
scenario is \emph{cross-silo} -- few clients, each holding large amounts of
data~\cite{li2020}. \emph{Differential privacy} (DP)~\cite{abadi2016} answers
the second: per-sample gradient clipping and calibrated Gaussian noise bound the
contribution of any single image, with the privacy budget $\varepsilon$
quantified by a privacy accountant. Finally, field adoption demands
\emph{trustworthiness}: operators need to understand why a bunch is labelled
ripe or overripe, which Explainable AI (XAI) methods such as
Grad-CAM++~\cite{gradcampp} provide and which faithfulness metrics can quantify.

This paper presents \textbf{FedX-Palm}, a framework integrating these four
pillars -- HFL, DP, a YOLOv11n detector, and Grad-CAM++ -- and, more
importantly, an honest empirical characterization of \emph{when the combination
works and when it does not}. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a leakage-free, bunch-identity split protocol for palm detection datasets,
and a federated baseline that quantifies the cost of federation (0.049 mAP@0.5)
under Non-IID partitions;
\item a systematic privacy--utility study over a $5 \times 5$ grid of client
count $K$ and noise multiplier $\sigma$, with $\varepsilon$ derived explicitly
from a PRV accountant, comparing full-model versus partial (frozen-backbone)
DP-SGD;
\item three empirical findings -- DP degradation is gradual not catastrophic,
larger $K$ worsens per-sample-DP utility (opposite to client-level DP), and
partial DP does not outperform full DP here -- that reframe earlier
``catastrophic collapse'' reports as configuration-specific artefacts; and
\item a per-class Grad-CAM++ faithfulness evaluation on the deployable operating
model, showing federation does not degrade explanation quality.
\end{enumerate}

Concretely, this study tests four hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{H1}] (\emph{FL viability}) A federated YOLOv11n--GroupNorm
detector trained without DP can reach the \emph{acceptable} accuracy band
(mAP@0.5 $\geq 0.70$) under Non-IID Dirichlet partitions, at a small cost
relative to centralized training.
\item[\textbf{H2}] (\emph{Gradual DP decay}) On a converged, generic-pretrained
object detector, per-sample DP-SGD induces a \emph{gradual} privacy--utility
decay across the practical range $\varepsilon \in [0.2, 14]$, rather than a
single-noise-level cliff.
\item[\textbf{H2-K}] (\emph{Client-count penalty}) Under per-sample DP-SGD,
increasing the number of clients $K$ at fixed $\sigma$ \emph{worsens} both
utility and the per-client $\varepsilon$, opposite to the intuition of
client-level DP where more clients amplify privacy.
\item[\textbf{H3}] (\emph{Explanation preservation}) Federation does not
materially degrade Grad-CAM++ faithfulness on the deployable model; FRR (and
its per-class breakdown) is at least preserved relative to centralized
training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Vision-Based Oil Palm Ripeness Assessment}
Colour-feature classifiers~\cite{sabri2017} preceded modern CNN approaches.
Junos \emph{et al.}~\cite{junos2022} applied a YOLO variant on UAV imagery for
fruit-level detection; Gunawan \emph{et al.}~\cite{gunawan2023} benchmarked
YOLOv8 variants for palm ripeness; Elwirehardja \emph{et
al.}~\cite{elwirehardja2021} deployed ripeness classifiers on mobile devices. A
common limitation is the assumption of centralized training, single-number mAP
validation, and silence on both privacy and explanation trustworthiness. This
work addresses all three.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Cross-Silo Scenarios}
McMahan \emph{et al.}~\cite{fedavg} introduced FedAvg: clients perform local SGD
and the server aggregates updates by a data-size-weighted average; see
\cite{li2020} for a survey. Kairouz \emph{et al.}~\cite{kairouz2021} distinguish
\emph{cross-device} from \emph{cross-silo}; a plantation consortium is clearly
cross-silo. Data heterogeneity is commonly simulated via Dirichlet
partitioning~\cite{hsu2019}, where a concentration parameter $\alpha$ controls
per-client class imbalance.

\subsection{Differential Privacy for Deep Learning}
DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016} provides $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP by clipping each
per-sample gradient to a norm bound $C$ and adding Gaussian noise of scale
$\sigma C$; the cumulative budget $\varepsilon$ is tracked by a privacy
accountant. Modern accountants based on the privacy loss distribution
(PRV)~\cite{gopi2021} give tight bounds and are implemented in
Opacus~\cite{opacus2021}. Because per-sample gradients require
per-sample-independent normalization, BatchNorm must be replaced by
GroupNorm~\cite{groupnorm}. Tram\`er and Boneh~\cite{tramer2021} argue that
DP-SGD benefits markedly from strong pre-trained features, suggesting that
training only a small head over a frozen backbone (partial DP) should be more
noise-efficient than full-model DP -- a hypothesis we test directly.

\subsection{Explainable AI and Faithfulness Metrics}
Grad-CAM~\cite{gradcam} weights each feature-map channel by the
spatially-averaged gradient of the target-class score; Grad-CAM++~\cite{gradcampp}
refines this for multiple same-class instances, common in FFB imagery. Two
faithfulness metrics are used here: an \emph{Occlusion Confidence Drop} (OCD)
and the \emph{Focus Retention Rate} (FRR), both in the perturbation-based
family~\cite{rise}; OCD is a deletion-based variant of, but distinct from, the
restriction-based \emph{Average Drop} of Chattopadhyay \emph{et
al.}~\cite{gradcampp} (we discuss the difference in Sec.~3.6). FRR
additionally serves as an agronomic alignment proxy -- does the model attend
to the fruit or the background?

\section{Methodology}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Anti-Leakage Split}
The dataset is the \emph{palm-fruit-ripeness-detection} collection (Roboflow,
version 2): 10,814 images from 89 unique bunches, annotated across six classes.
A naive random split risks leakage at the bunch level -- multiple frames of the
same bunch could land in both train and test, letting the model recognize the
bunch rather than learn ripeness cues. We apply a stratified group split keyed
by \texttt{bunch\_id}: each bunch is assigned entirely to one partition, with
stratification preserving class balance (Table~\ref{tab:split}). A leakage audit
confirmed zero bunch-identity overlap between any two partitions.

\subsection{Non-IID Federation and Detector}
The training partition is divided among $K$ clients using a Dirichlet
distribution with per-client concentration $\alpha$ spanning 0.1 (highly skewed)
to 0.8 (near uniform), simulating realistic estate heterogeneity. Validation and
test sets are kept global for comparability. The detector is YOLOv11n
($\approx$2.6M parameters), initialized from COCO~\cite{coco} weights; all 81
BatchNorm layers are converted in place to GroupNorm~\cite{groupnorm} (eight
groups, auto-reduced as needed), with affine parameters copied to preserve the
pre-trained initialization. This conversion is mandatory for per-sample DP-SGD
and is applied uniformly across all experiments for consistency.

\subsection{Federated Training (FedAvg)}
Training uses FedAvg over $T$ communication rounds. Each round, every client
receives global parameters $w_t$, runs $E = 2$ local SGD epochs (learning rate
0.01, momentum 0.937, weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$, batch 16, image $640\times
640$), and returns $w_t^k$; the server aggregates by data-size weighting,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:fedavg}):
\begin{equation}
w_{t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n}\, w_t^k,
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}
with $n_k$ the shard size of client $k$ and $n=\sum_k n_k$. The federated
baseline (B2) uses $K=4$; an extended 25-round run provides the deployment
operating point (50 epoch-equivalents, matching the 50-epoch centralized
reference B1 for a fair federation-cost comparison).

\subsection{Per-Sample DP-SGD and Privacy Accounting}
For the privacy experiments, local optimization is replaced by per-sample
DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016}. For each sample $i$ in a Poisson-sampled minibatch,
the gradient $g_i$ is clipped to norm $C$ and Gaussian noise is added before
the update, as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:dpsgd}):
\begin{equation}
\tilde{g} = \frac{1}{B}\!\left( \sum_{i} g_i \cdot
\min\!\Big(1, \tfrac{C}{\lVert g_i\rVert_2}\Big) +
\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 \mathbf{I}) \right).
\label{eq:dpsgd}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{DP hyperparameters.} Table~\ref{tab:dphyp} lists the exact DP
hyperparameters used; they are held fixed across the entire $K \times \sigma$
grid so that comparisons isolate the effect of $K$ and $\sigma$ alone. The
per-sample clipping norm is $C = 1.0$ (a standard mid-range choice for vision
detectors), the local minibatch size is $B = 16$ (identical to the non-private
baselines, ensuring a fair utility comparison), and Poisson sub-sampling is
applied within each client. The sampling rate is therefore \emph{per client},
$q_k = B / n_k$, so clients with smaller shards $n_k$ see larger $q_k$ and
spend privacy faster -- this asymmetry is the source of the H2-K
``double-penalty'' analyzed in Sec.~\ref{sec:e1results}.

\paragraph{Privacy accounting (per-client, not global).} A separate Opacus
\texttt{PrivacyEngine}~\cite{opacus2021} is attached to every client's
optimizer; consequently the privacy accounting is \emph{per-client}, not
global. Each client's PRV accountant~\cite{gopi2021} composes the per-step
sub-sampled Gaussian mechanism over its own $T \cdot E$ local steps to yield
$\varepsilon_k$ at fixed $\delta = 10^{-5}$. Reported $\varepsilon$ values
correspond to the smallest-shard client (the worst case under H2-K). We do
\emph{not} compose across clients into a single global $\varepsilon$: the
shared global model is updated by FedAvg over disjoint client shards, so each
client's neighbourhood is its own dataset and the per-client guarantee is the
relevant DP statement under the standard horizontal-FL threat model.

Two DP variants are studied: \emph{full} DP-SGD (E1, all $\approx$2.6M
parameters trainable) and \emph{partial} DP-SGD (E2, backbone frozen, only the
$\approx$0.2M-parameter head trained), the latter testing the Tram\`er--Boneh
hypothesis~\cite{tramer2021}. Each variant is swept over $K \in
\{2,4,8,12,16\} \times \sigma \in \{0.5,1,1.5,2,3\}$, giving 25 configurations
per variant.

\subsection{Explanation Quality Metrics}
Grad-CAM++ is applied at the last shared convolutional layer before the
detection head. On $N = 200$ test images, two faithfulness metrics are
computed globally and per class. The \emph{primary} metric is the Focus
Retention Rate, which is well-defined and unambiguous; the second is an
occlusion-based confidence-drop signal that we deliberately rename to avoid
confusion with the literature.

\paragraph{Focus Retention Rate (FRR).} FRR is the fraction of heatmap
intensity falling inside the ground-truth boxes, as in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:frr}); higher is better:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FRR} = \frac{\sum_{p\in \mathrm{ROI}} I(p)}
                    {\sum_{p\in \mathrm{Image}} I(p)}.
\label{eq:frr}
\end{equation}
FRR doubles as an agronomic alignment proxy: does the model attend to the
fruit, or to the background? It is the metric on which the central
explanation claims of this paper rest.

\paragraph{Occlusion Confidence Drop (OCD).} We complement FRR with an
occlusion-based signal that we name \emph{Occlusion Confidence Drop} (OCD)
rather than \emph{Average Drop} to avoid clashing with the original
Chattopadhyay \emph{et al.}~\cite{gradcampp} definition (where the input is
\emph{restricted} to the salient region and lower drop is better). Here, in
contrast, the salient region (heatmap $\geq 0.5$) is \emph{occluded} by
replacing it with the per-image mean pixel value, and OCD measures the
resulting relative confidence drop, as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:ad}); higher is
better, because a large drop means the occluded region was decisive:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OCD} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{\max(0,\, Y_i^c - O_i^c)}{Y_i^c}\times 100\%,
\label{eq:ad}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i^c$ and $O_i^c$ are the model's confidences on the original and
salient-region-occluded image $i$ for class $c$. OCD is a deletion-based
faithfulness signal in the same broad family as RISE~\cite{rise}, but
formally distinct from Chattopadhyay's restriction-based Average Drop.

\subsection{Federation Cost without Privacy Noise}

reference. The federated detector reaches mAP@0.5 = 0.738, only 0.049 ($\approx$6\%)
below the centralized upper bound of 0.787, with both in the acceptable range
($\geq 0.70$). Federation is therefore a viable basis for decision support: the
utility cost of keeping raw imagery on-site is small. This validates hypothesis
H1 (the GroupNorm baseline reaches acceptable accuracy) and establishes the
reference against which the cost of privacy is measured.

\subsection{Privacy--Utility Trade-off under Full DP-SGD (E1)}
\label{sec:e1results}
Table~\ref{tab:e1} reports mAP@0.5 across the full $K\times\sigma$ grid, and
Table~\ref{tab:eps} the corresponding $\varepsilon$ from the PRV accountant.
Three findings stand out. First, no configuration reaches the acceptable
threshold: the best point is $K{=}4,\sigma{=}0.5$ at mAP@0.5 = 0.190
($\varepsilon = 8.36$), so the cost of formal privacy ($0.738 - 0.190 = 0.548$)
vastly exceeds the cost of federation (0.049). Second, degradation is
\emph{gradual}: along any row, utility declines smoothly as $\sigma$ rises and
$\varepsilon$ falls, with no single noise level triggering a vertical collapse
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pu}). This refutes earlier ``catastrophic-collapse'' readings
that stemmed from a BatchNorm--Opacus pipeline artefact rather than a true
privacy--utility cliff (H2 confirmed). Third, larger $K$ \emph{consistently
worsens} utility at matched $\sigma$ ($K{=}2$ gives 0.188 vs $K{=}16$ at 0.087
for $\sigma{=}0.5$), and simultaneously inflates $\varepsilon$ (8.62 vs 13.75)
because smaller shards raise the sampling rate $q = B/n_k$ -- a per-sample-DP
``double penalty'' opposite to the client-level-DP intuition that more clients
help (H2-K confirmed).

\subsection{Partial DP-SGD (E2) and the Limits of Frozen-Backbone Transfer}
\label{sec:tramer-scope}
Freezing the backbone and training only the detection head under DP-SGD was
expected to be more noise-efficient: with far fewer trainable parameters, the
relative scale of the privacy noise should fall and a small head should
suffice when the frozen features are task-relevant -- the central intuition of
Tram\`er and Boneh~\cite{tramer2021}. Empirically, on \emph{our} setting, the
opposite holds: across every matched $(K,\sigma)$, full DP (E1) outperforms
partial DP (E2) -- E2's best point is only mAP@0.5 = 0.113
($K{=}2,\sigma{=}0.5$) versus E1's 0.190, an average gap of about $+0.08$
mAP@0.5 in favour of E1 (Table~\ref{tab:e1vse2}). The most likely cause is
\emph{domain mismatch with the frozen features}: COCO-pretrained YOLOv11n
features encode generic object cues (edges, textures, shapes) but not
FFB-specific ones (mesocarp colour, bunch geometry, fruitlet occlusion), so a
$\approx$0.2M-parameter head alone cannot bridge the capacity gap to
six-class palm ripeness. This does not refute Tram\`er and Boneh's broader
claim, whose evidence is drawn from image-classification benchmarks with
strong, in-domain pre-training (e.g., ImageNet-pretrained ResNets on
ImageNet-like targets); rather, it identifies an \emph{empirical scope limit}
of frozen-backbone DP for object detectors transferred to a new visual domain.
We therefore present E2 not as a refutation, but as a configuration-specific
counter-example that motivates investigating in-domain pre-training or
\emph{adapter}-based partial DP as future work.

\subsection{Three Operating Regimes}
Synthesizing B1, B2, E1, and E2 yields three regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regime}).
The \emph{no-formal-privacy} regime (centralized 0.787; federated 0.738) is the
only one that is deployable; the \emph{weak-DP} regime ($\varepsilon\approx 8$,
mAP$\approx$0.19) is degraded but non-trivial; the \emph{strict-DP} regime
($\varepsilon < 0.5$) collapses. Practically: for threats centred on raw-data
exfiltration, federated no-DP is recommended; for scenarios requiring formal
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP, the present trade-off is too costly and motivates
DP-friendly architectures or substantially larger communication budgets.

\subsection{Explanation Faithfulness on the Operating Model}
Grad-CAM++ is evaluated on the deployable federated model (B2). Per class
(Table~\ref{tab:xai}), the colour-driven classes \emph{Unripe} and \emph{Empty
Bunch} are the most tightly localized on the fruit (FRR $\approx$ 0.46), while
\emph{Ripe} and \emph{Underripe} show the highest occlusion sensitivity (OCD
$\approx$ 26\%). \emph{Abnormal} is weakest on both (OCD = 7.45\%, FRR =
0.093): lacking a single consistent cue, the model's attention disperses onto
the canopy, so \emph{Abnormal} predictions warrant manual confirmation. The
\textbf{primary} explanation claim anchors on FRR -- the structurally cleaner
metric: applying the identical protocol to the centralized model gives global
FRR = 0.182 versus the federated 0.281, so federation does \emph{not} degrade
explanation quality and, on this metric, slightly sharpens it. OCD shows the
same direction (4.95\% centralized, 15.90\% federated). Both signals agree on
the hardest case as well -- \emph{Abnormal} has the lowest FRR under either
regime -- confirming that the difficulty is intrinsic to the class rather than
an artefact of federation. H3 is therefore confirmed for the operating model.
Representative heatmaps appear in Fig.~\ref{fig:xai}.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
All grid runs use a single seed under a compute budget; reported numbers may
vary by $\pm$0.01--0.03 mAP@0.5, though the qualitative trends (gradual DP
decay, $K$-monotonicity, E1$>$E2) have effect sizes well beyond that range. A
single dataset source bounds external generalization; multi-estate validation
is future work. Best-round selection per run mildly favours the DP variants but
is applied uniformly. FRR rewards in-box heatmaps and may under-credit
explanations attending to nearby cues such as fallen fruitlets; per-class OCD
is a complementary signal that mostly agrees with FRR on the ranking.

\section{Deployment Demonstration}
\label{sec:deployment}
%==============================================================================
To establish operational viability beyond simulation, the deployable operating
checkpoint (B2 federated $K{=}4$, 25 rounds, mAP@0.5 = 0.738) is packaged into
a Docker image and served from a CPU-only Virtual Private Server (VPS). The
service exposes a Flask \texttt{/predict} endpoint and a lightweight web
front-end through which an operator uploads a plantation image and receives
three artefacts simultaneously: bounding boxes with class labels and
confidences, the per-image Grad-CAM++ heatmap, and a tabular summary of
detections (Fig.~\ref{fig:vps}).

Operationally, the demonstration validates three architectural commitments of
FedX-Palm. \emph{First}, separation of training and inference: while federated
training requires per-client GPU resources, inference runs comfortably on a
commodity CPU at roughly two seconds per image (1.2~s YOLOv11n forward pass
plus 0.8~s Grad-CAM++), keeping production infrastructure costs low.
\emph{Second}, instantiation of zero-trust data flow: only the FedAvg-aggregated
model weights leave the client estates; no raw imagery is stored on the
inference VPS, mirroring the privacy architecture analyzed throughout the
paper. \emph{Third}, end-user trust support through XAI: by surfacing the
Grad-CAM++ heatmap alongside each detection, the service lets field operators
visually verify whether a ``ripe'' or ``overripe'' label is grounded in the
fruit region or, as flagged for the \emph{Abnormal} class in
Sec.~4.6, in surrounding context warranting manual confirmation.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
FedX-Palm integrates horizontal federated learning, per-sample differential
privacy, a YOLOv11n--GroupNorm detector, and Grad-CAM++ explainability for
six-class oil palm FFB ripeness detection, and characterizes honestly where the
combination is practical. On a leakage-free bunch-identity split, federation
costs only 0.049 mAP@0.5 (0.738 vs 0.787), making privacy-by-architecture
attractive. Formal differential privacy, however, is expensive on this detector:
across a $5\times5$ $K\times\sigma$ grid with $\varepsilon$ from a PRV
accountant, the best full-DP point reaches mAP@0.5 = 0.190 at $\varepsilon =
8.36$, degradation is gradual rather than catastrophic, larger $K$ consistently
hurts (a per-sample-DP penalty), and partial DP does not help. Grad-CAM++
faithfulness on the deployable model is preserved under federation (global FRR
= 0.281 vs 0.182 centralized; complementary OCD signal in agreement), with the
Abnormal class flagged for manual oversight. A Docker-packaged inference
service on a commodity CPU VPS demonstrates that the federated operating model
runs end-to-end with visual XAI in roughly two seconds per image.
These results both demonstrate a working privacy-aware, explainable palm
ripeness system and delineate the boundary at which formal DP becomes
impractical -- motivating DP-friendly architectures and larger communication
budgets as future work, alongside multi-estate validation and edge-deployment
benchmarks.
