\begin{center}
\textbf{ABSTRACT}
\end{center}
\noindent
Timely harvesting of oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFB) is decisive for oil
yield and quality, yet manual ripeness assessment is subjective and
inconsistent. Computer-vision automation promises consistency, but centrally
aggregating plantation imagery conflicts with the confidentiality of operational
data. This study investigates whether a \textit{Federated Learning} (FL)
approach -- training a shared model without moving raw imagery across estates
-- can deliver six-class FFB ripeness detection that is both \emph{accurate}
and \emph{explainable}, making it a trustworthy harvest decision-support tool.
A YOLOv11n detector is trained federatively using FedAvg over five
communication rounds on Non-IID Dirichlet partitions derived from a
leakage-free bunch-identity split (training: 9,094 images from 72 bunches;
validation: 769 images from 8 bunches; test: 951 images from 9 bunches). All
BatchNorm layers are converted to GroupNorm for cross-experiment consistency.
The explanation quality is assessed with Grad-CAM++ via per-class
\textit{Average Drop} (AD) and \textit{Focus Retention Rate} (FRR), the latter
doubling as an agronomic alignment proxy that measures heatmap intensity
within fruit bounding boxes. The federated model attains mAP@0.5 = \tbd{B2
mAP50} (centralized reference: 0.787), with global AD = \tbd{AD}\% and global
FRR = \tbd{FRR}. The per-class analysis reveals that \tbd{best/worst class}.
The results indicate FL can deliver privacy-preserving palm ripeness detection
without sacrificing decision explainability.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} explainable artificial intelligence, federated
learning, Grad-CAM++, oil palm ripeness, YOLOv11.

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
The oil palm industry is a cornerstone of Indonesian and Malaysian
agribusiness, with the two countries together accounting for over 85\% of the
world's palm oil supply~\cite{usda2024}. At the operational level, the single
most influential decision in determining oil yield and quality is the timing
of fresh fruit bunch (FFB) harvest. Bunches harvested too early contain
insufficient mesocarp oil, whereas bunches harvested too late accumulate Free
Fatty Acid (FFA), degrading Crude Palm Oil (CPO) quality. Industry practice
distinguishes six commercially relevant ripeness classes: \textit{Unripe},
\textit{Underripe}, \textit{Ripe}, \textit{Overripe}, \textit{Empty Bunch},
and \textit{Abnormal}~\cite{junos2022}. Traditional assessment by harvest
foremen, based on bunch color and loose fruitlet count, is subjective and has
reported misclassification rates of 15--25\% under suboptimal
lighting~\cite{sabri2017}.

Modern object detectors such as YOLOv11 can localize and classify multiple
bunches in a single frame in real time, making vision-based automation
feasible at the plantation scale~\cite{yolov11}. However, training accurate
detectors requires diverse imagery spanning seasons, lighting, and geographic
conditions. Centralizing such imagery clashes with a practical reality:
plantation images are \emph{commercially sensitive} (revealing block
locations, production volumes, and operational practices), and estates are
reluctant to ship raw data to a shared server.

\textit{Federated Learning} (FL)~\cite{fedavg} provides an architectural
answer: multiple estates collaboratively train a shared model while raw
imagery remains on local infrastructure; only model parameters traverse the
network. In the taxonomy of Kairouz~\textit{et al.}~\cite{kairouz2021}, the
plantation-consortium scenario is \textit{cross-silo} -- few clients, each
holding large amounts of data, with stable availability.

Accuracy alone, however, is insufficient for field adoption. An agricultural
decision-support system must be \emph{trustworthy}: operators need to
understand \emph{why} the model labels a bunch as ``ripe'' or ``overripe.''
Without auditable visual explanations, predictions risk being either rejected
outright or trusted uncritically -- both failure modes carry real economic
cost. \textit{Explainable AI} (XAI) methods such as Grad-CAM++~\cite{gradcampp}
generate heatmaps highlighting the image regions most responsible for a
prediction, and the quality of such heatmaps can be measured quantitatively
through \emph{faithfulness} metrics.

Distinct from the authors' prior work that characterizes the behavior of
\textit{Differential Privacy} (DP) on this detector~\cite{paperA}, the present
study addresses an \emph{application-oriented} question: \textbf{can federated
learning deliver oil palm ripeness detection that is simultaneously accurate
and explainable enough to serve as a credible harvest decision-support tool?}

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item An \emph{in-depth, per-class evaluation} of Grad-CAM++ faithfulness on
a federated palm ripeness detector, going beyond a single global metric.
\item A \emph{bunch-identity-based anti-leakage split protocol} for palm
detection datasets, preventing per-bunch leakage that otherwise inflates
metrics.
\item Empirical evidence that federation \emph{does not significantly degrade}
the quality of model explanations relative to centralized training, supporting
the practical viability of FL for privacy-aware agricultural decision support.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews the
relevant literature. Section~3 details the dataset, federation, model, and
faithfulness metrics. Section~4 reports the per-class explainability results
together with detection accuracy and the federated-versus-centralized
comparison. Section~5 concludes.

\section{Related Work}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Vision-Based Oil Palm Ripeness Assessment}
Several studies have investigated automated palm ripeness assessment using
deep learning. Color-feature classifiers~\cite{sabri2017} preceded modern CNN
approaches. Junos~\textit{et al.}~\cite{junos2022} applied a YOLO variant on
UAV imagery for fruit-level detection. \tbd{add 2--3 more recent studies with
brief one-line summaries}. A common limitation across these works is the
assumption of \emph{centralized} training and the use of only a single
top-1/mAP number to validate the model -- neither the trustworthiness of the
visual explanation nor the privacy implications of centralized data
aggregation is examined. The present work addresses both gaps.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Cross-Silo Scenarios}
McMahan~\textit{et al.}~\cite{fedavg} introduced the canonical FedAvg
algorithm: clients perform local stochastic gradient descent and the server
aggregates updates by a data-size-weighted average. Kairouz~\textit{et
al.}~\cite{kairouz2021} distinguish \textit{cross-device} (millions of
unreliable clients with little data each) from \textit{cross-silo} (few
reliable clients with large data each) -- a plantation consortium clearly
falls in the latter regime. Data heterogeneity between sites is commonly
simulated via Dirichlet partitioning~\cite{hsu2019}, where a concentration
parameter $\alpha$ controls class imbalance per client.

\subsection{Explainable AI and Faithfulness Metrics}
Grad-CAM~\cite{gradcam} weights each feature map channel by the
spatially-averaged gradient of the target class score, producing a class
activation heatmap. Grad-CAM++~\cite{gradcampp} refines this by using a
weighted combination of positive partial derivatives, providing more accurate
localization when \emph{multiple instances} of the same class are present --
a condition that routinely occurs in FFB imagery containing several bunches
per frame.

Two complementary faithfulness metrics are used in this work. The
\textit{Average Drop} (AD) measures the mean reduction in model confidence
when the input is restricted to the heatmap-highlighted region. A low AD
indicates that the highlighted region is genuinely the basis for the
prediction. The \textit{Focus Retention Rate} (FRR), defined as the fraction
of total heatmap intensity falling inside the ground-truth fruit bounding
boxes, measures whether the model attends to the fruit rather than the
background. FRR thus serves a dual role as both a faithfulness metric and an
agronomic alignment proxy. Both metrics are documented in the thesis
formulation of Chattopadhyay~\textit{et al.}~\cite{gradcampp} and have been
widely adopted in detector explainability evaluation.

\section{Methodology}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Anti-Leakage Split}
The dataset is the \emph{palm-fruit-ripeness-detection} collection (Roboflow,
version 2), comprising 10,814 images from 89 unique bunches, with bounding-box
annotations across six classes in alphabetical order: \textit{Abnormal},
\textit{Empty Bunch}, \textit{Overripe}, \textit{Ripe}, \textit{Underripe},
\textit{Unripe}.

A naive random split risks leakage at the \emph{bunch level}: multiple frames
of the same bunch (different angles or distances) could be assigned to
\emph{both} training and test partitions, allowing the model to ``recognize
the bunch'' rather than learn generalizable ripeness cues. To prevent this, we
apply a \textbf{stratified group split keyed by \textit{bunch\_id}}: each
bunch is assigned entirely to one partition, with stratification preserving
class balance across partitions. The resulting split is shown in
Table~\ref{tab:split}. A leakage audit confirmed zero bunch-identity overlap
between any two partitions.

\subsection{Non-IID Federation Setup}
The training partition is divided among \tbd{K, e.g., 4} clients using a
Dirichlet distribution with per-client concentration parameters spanning from
$\alpha = 0.1$ (highly skewed -- a client dominated by one or two classes) to
$\alpha = 0.8$ (close to uniform). This protocol simulates realistic
heterogeneity across estates differing in cultivar, harvest schedule, and
management practice. The validation and test partitions are kept
\emph{global} (shared across all clients) so that metrics are directly
comparable across configurations.

\subsection{Detector and Architectural Modification}
The base detector is YOLOv11n (approximately 2.6M parameters), initialized
from COCO pre-trained weights. All BatchNorm layers (81 in YOLOv11n) are
converted in-place to GroupNorm (eight groups; the count auto-reduces when it
does not divide a layer's channel count). The affine parameters of each
BatchNorm are copied to the corresponding GroupNorm to preserve the benefit
of pre-trained initialization. This conversion ensures consistency with the
companion DP study~\cite{paperA} in which per-sample gradient computation
requires per-sample-independent normalization. After conversion, validation
confirms zero remaining BatchNorm layers.

\subsection{Federated Training Procedure}
Training uses FedAvg over $T = 5$ communication rounds. In each round, every
client receives the current global parameters $w_t$, performs $E = 2$ local
epochs of SGD on its local shard (learning rate $0.01$, momentum $0.937$,
weight decay $5 \times 10^{-4}$, batch size 16, image size $640 \times 640$),
and returns updated parameters $w_t^k$. The server aggregates by data-size
weighting,
\begin{equation}
w_{t+1} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{n_k}{n}\, w_t^k,
\label{eq:fedavg}
\end{equation}
where $n_k$ is the local shard size of client $k$ and $n = \sum_k n_k$. The
loss is the standard YOLOv11 composite of CIoU box loss (weight 7.5),
classification loss (weight 0.5), and Distribution Focal Loss (weight 1.5).
A centralized reference is trained for 50 epochs on the entire training
partition under identical hyperparameters.

Formal privacy (Differential Privacy) is treated and analyzed in detail in
the authors' companion paper~\cite{paperA}; here, privacy is addressed at the
architectural level (raw imagery never traverses the network), keeping the
focus on explainability.

\subsection{Explanation Quality Metrics}
Grad-CAM++ is applied at the last convolutional layer preceding the detection
head. Two faithfulness metrics are computed on $N = $~\tbd{e.g., 120} test
images, both globally and per-class.

\textit{Average Drop} (AD), expressed as a percentage, quantifies how much
confidence the model loses when the input is restricted to the heatmap region
(lower is better):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AD} \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\max(0,\, Y_i^c - O_i^c)}{Y_i^c} \times 100\%,
\label{eq:ad}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i^c$ is the model's confidence on the original image $i$ for class
$c$, and $O_i^c$ is its confidence when only the masked (heatmap-highlighted)
region is visible.

\textit{Focus Retention Rate} (FRR) quantifies what fraction of heatmap
intensity falls inside the ground-truth fruit bounding boxes (higher is
better):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FRR} \;=\; \frac{\sum_{p \in \mathrm{ROI}} I(p)}{\sum_{p \in \mathrm{Image}} I(p)},
\label{eq:frr}
\end{equation}
where $I(p)$ is the heatmap intensity at pixel $p$ and ROI is the union of
ground-truth bounding boxes for the target class. FRR thus simultaneously
measures faithfulness and \emph{agronomic alignment}: does the model attend
to the fruit, or to the background? Per-class breakdowns identify which
ripeness classes are most reliably explained and which deserve operator
caution.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\textit{Internal.} A single dataset source (Roboflow) bounds generalization;
multi-estate validation is left to future work. \textit{Construct.} Heatmap
faithfulness is one operationalization of ``trustworthiness''; complementary
user studies with harvest foremen would strengthen the agronomic claim.
\textit{External.} FRR rewards heatmaps concentrated inside bounding boxes,
which may under-credit explanations correctly attending to nearby visual
cues such as loose fruitlets on the ground; the per-class AD provides a
complementary signal.

\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
This paper investigated whether \textit{Federated Learning} can deliver
six-class oil palm ripeness detection that is simultaneously accurate and
explainable enough to serve as a trustworthy harvest decision-support tool.
On a leakage-free \textit{bunch\_id}-stratified split (9,094 training / 769
validation / 951 test images), a federated YOLOv11n-GroupNorm detector trained
with FedAvg attains mAP@0.5 = \tbd{B2 mAP50} -- a \tbd{small/modest/large}
gap from the centralized reference (0.787). The Grad-CAM++ faithfulness
evaluation, broken down per ripeness class, yields global AD = \tbd{}\% and
FRR = \tbd{}, with \tbd{class} being the most reliably explained and
\tbd{class} the most challenging. Federation \tbd{does not materially / only
mildly} degrade explanation quality, supporting the practical viability of
privacy-preserving collaborative training in plantation consortia.

Future work includes integration of per-sample Differential Privacy (the
detailed study of which is reported in the authors' companion
work~\cite{paperA}), validation on multi-estate datasets spanning cultivars
and climates, and edge deployment benchmarks for in-field inference.
